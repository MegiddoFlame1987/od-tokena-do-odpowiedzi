From Signal to Internet

Every prompt you send to a model travels as a signal. It is a voltage in a cable, a flash of light in glass or a radio wave in the air. On the way it passes your router, your internet provider and several networks you have never heard of. This page follows that trip one step at a time. Two questions come up at every step: who can see your data here, and who can reach your computer.

Each section ends with a command to try on your own computer. Every address in the demos comes from a range set aside for examples, so it never points at a real machine. State of play: 11 November 2026.

Part I

Signal

A bit is only a 0 or a 1. To travel, it has to become something physical and reach the next box in the room.

1 / 10

A bit becomes a signal

The same bits look different in each medium. In copper they are a change of voltage, in glass a flash of light, in the air a change in a radio wave. Pick a medium and watch the same eight bits.

Try it: on Windows run netsh wlan show interfaces. It shows your Wi-Fi band, signal strength and link speed. On a Mac, hold Option and click the Wi-Fi icon.

Verified: copper Ethernet carries bits as voltage, fibre as pulses of light, Wi-Fi as changes in a radio wave (IEEE 802.3 and 802.11). One copper cable run is specified for up to 100 metres. Simplified: real links never send a plain on and off. They pack several bits into one change of the signal, which is why speeds are so high.
2 / 10

Your home network: frames and MAC addresses

Inside your home, devices talk in frames. A frame is a small envelope with the sender and the receiver written on the outside. Those two labels are MAC addresses: a fixed address of each network card. Pick a device and send a frame to the router.

Try it: run arp -a on Windows, Mac or Linux. It lists the devices your computer talked to recently, with their MAC addresses.

Verified: a MAC address has 48 bits. In a factory address the first 24 bits name the maker of the card (IEEE). Phones now usually use a random MAC address for each Wi-Fi network, which makes them harder to follow. Simplified: the demo treats the router, the switch and the Wi-Fi point as one box. In most homes one box from the provider does all three jobs.
Part II

Address

A MAC address works only inside one network. To cross the internet, every packet needs an address the whole world understands, and a router that guards the door.

3 / 10

IP address: where on the internet

An IP address is the delivery address of a packet. A packet is one small piece of a message, with that address written on it. Your router gives private addresses to devices at home and holds one public address for the outside world. Type an address to see which kind it is.

Try it: run ipconfig on Windows, or ip addr on Linux, to see the private address of your computer. Then open the status page of your router and find its internet address (often called WAN). If it starts with 100.64 up to 100.127, your provider shares one public address between many customers.

Verified: an IPv4 address has 32 bits, about 4.3 billion addresses in total (RFC 791). The private ranges are fixed in RFC 1918, the range shared by providers in RFC 6598, the example ranges in RFC 5737. Simplified: the newer IPv6 has 128 bits, enough for every device to have its own public address (RFC 8200). Many networks now run both side by side. The demo checks only IPv4.
4 / 10

NAT and the firewall: why the outside cannot knock

The whole home shares one public address. NAT is the trick that makes it work: the router keeps a table of which device inside started which conversation. An answer that matches a row comes in. Anything else is dropped. Start a few conversations, then let a stranger knock.

InsidePublic sideTalking to

Try it: open the page of your router (often http://192.168.1.1) and find Port Mapping and DMZ. Both lists should be empty, unless you know exactly why a row is there.

Verified: NAT rewrites the address and port of outgoing packets and keeps a table to match the answers (RFC 3022). Port forwarding adds a permanent row for everyone. DMZ sends every packet without a row to one device. Simplified: in a real router the firewall and NAT are separate rules, shown here as one. Practical: nothing in this material needs a change in your router. The laptop, the mail check and the model API all call out.
Part III

Names and delivery

People remember names, machines need numbers. Then the message has to arrive whole, at the right program.

5 / 10

DNS: names into numbers

DNS is the phone book of the internet. You type a name and a resolver, a server that looks names up for you, asks a chain of servers: first the root, then the servers for the ending such as .com or .app, then the servers that own the name. If the name does not exist, the answer is NXDOMAIN. Type a name, or try the real typo below.

    Try it: run nslookup from-token-to-answer.vercel.app, then nslookup from-token-to-answer.app. The first gives an address. The second says the name does not exist.

    Verified: DNS is a tree with the root at the top, read from the right end of the name (RFC 1034 and 1035). NXDOMAIN means the name does not exist. Answers are kept for a set time, so the full chain runs only on the first visit. Simplified: the addresses in the demo are made up. Practical: ordinary DNS questions are not encrypted, so your provider can see which names you look up, even when the page itself is locked.
    6 / 10

    Packets, TCP and ports

    A long message is cut into packets, and some get lost on the way. TCP numbers them, the receiver confirms what arrived and asks again for what is missing. UDP just sends and never asks. Send the same message both ways and lose one packet.

    A port is a number that says which program on the machine a packet is for. Ports in this material: 443 for web pages and model APIs, 993 for reading mail over a locked connection, 53 for DNS, 22 for remote control of a server.

    Try it: on Windows run netstat -an | find "ESTABLISHED". On a Mac or Linux run netstat -an | grep ESTABLISHED. Each line is one open conversation, with its ports.

    Verified: TCP numbers the data, confirms receipt and sends again what was lost (RFC 9293). UDP sends without confirmation (RFC 768). Port numbers run from 0 to 65535. Simplified: TCP counts bytes, not whole packets, and opens every conversation with a three step handshake. The newer HTTP/3 runs over QUIC, which builds the same careful delivery on top of UDP (RFC 9000).
    Part IV

    On the way

    Between you and the model sit people and companies you do not know. Encryption decides what they can read. Distance decides how long you wait.

    7 / 10

    HTTPS: who sees what on the way

    TLS is the lock behind the padlock icon and the S in HTTPS. It encrypts the content between your computer and the server. Everyone in between still sees that you are talking, to whom, when and how much. Pick a connection and a person on the route.

    Try it: click the padlock or the site settings icon next to the address bar of your browser, then open the certificate. You see who issued it and until when it is valid.

    Verified: TLS 1.3 encrypts everything after the opening handshake and checks the certificate of the server against a trusted authority (RFC 8446). The name of the site is usually sent in the clear at the start (SNI, RFC 6066). Simplified: encrypted DNS and newer extensions can hide the name, but they are not used everywhere yet. Practical: the rule from tab 3 still stands. The lock protects the road, not the destination.
    8 / 10

    The internet is a network of networks

    No single company runs the internet. Tens of thousands of separate networks agree to pass packets for each other. Distance still costs time, because light in glass covers about 200,000 kilometres per second. Move the slider to see the shortest possible round trip.

    Try it: on Windows run tracert from-token-to-answer.vercel.app, on a Mac or Linux traceroute from-token-to-answer.vercel.app. Each line is one router on the way, with its delay. A line of stars is a router that chose not to answer.

    Verified: networks swap routes with each other using BGP (RFC 4271). Simplified: the slider assumes one straight cable and no waiting inside routers, so real round trips are longer. The speed is rounded: light in glass travels at about two thirds of its speed in empty space. Most traffic between continents runs through cables on the sea floor, not satellites. Logical: where the data centre stands changes the delay you feel. An agent that runs fifty steps pays the round trip fifty times.
    Part V

    The whole trip

    How to reach a machine at home without opening the door. Then one prompt, followed from the Enter key to the answer.

    9 / 10

    Tunnels and VPNs: reaching home without opening a door

    A tunnel wraps your packets inside other, encrypted packets. A VPN is a private network built from such tunnels. Port forwarding opens a door in the router for the whole internet. A tunnel works the other way round: both devices call out first, so the router never has to open anything. Compare the two.

      Try it: if you use Tailscale, run tailscale status. It lists your devices with their 100.x addresses. That is the shared range from section 3, reused inside the private network.

      Verified: Tailscale is built on WireGuard, which runs over UDP, and connects devices through NAT, with relays as a fallback when no direct path works (Tailscale documentation). Simplified: the coordination server knows which devices you have and their addresses, even though it cannot read the traffic. Practical: for reaching a home machine from outside, a tunnel is the safer default than an open port.

      One prompt, start to finish

      Everything together, on one case: your laptop sends a prompt to a model in a data centre. Step through it and watch who could read the prompt at each step.

      Try it: open the developer tools of your browser (F12), choose the Network tab and reload this page. Each row is one request that made the full trip.

      Verified: the order follows how browsers and API programs connect: DNS, then TCP or QUIC, then TLS, then the request. Simplified: the layers are not separate boxes in real life, and HTTP/3 joins steps 3 and 4 into one. The model address is made up. Practical: step 9 is the line that matters for work data.
