From Token to Answer

Part I

Bricks

Four things that everything further on is built from. If these four are clear, the rest is assembly.

1 / 20

Token

The model does not see letters or words. It sees tokens, pieces of text from a fixed vocabulary (usually 50 to 200 thousand entries). Type something and watch the split.

Verified: text is cut into tokens from a vocabulary, Polish needs more tokens than English. Simplified: the split in the demo is approximate. A real tokeniser, the program that cuts the text (BPE for example), splits differently, but about as densely.
2 / 20

Embedding

An embedding is a vector, a list of numbers, given to every token. Tokens with similar meaning land close together. Click a word and you will see its three nearest neighbours.

Click a word.
Verified: embeddings come out of training. Words used in similar ways land close together. Simplified: two dimensions instead of a few thousand. The example "king − man + woman ≈ queen" works in practice, though not always perfectly.
3 / 20

Weight and neuron

A neuron multiplies every input by a weight and adds the results up. Then it adds a constant (the bias) and passes the sum through an activation function. Here that is ReLU: it turns a negative result into zero. The weights are the numbers that hold what the model has learned. They are the only thing the model learns in training. Move the sliders.

Verified: the definition of a neuron and of ReLU. Important: the transformer is the architecture of current language models. Inside it a "neuron" sits one level below the operations people talk about (matrices, attention). The intuition carries over, the vocabulary does not always.
4 / 20

Layer

A layer is many neurons computed at once. In the arithmetic it is an input vector times a matrix, a table of weights. Three inputs and four outputs give twelve weights. Real layers have millions of them.

Part II

Architecture

How a transformer is built out of the bricks. One operation makes all the difference here: attention.

5 / 20

Attention

Attention is the mechanism in which every token looks at all the earlier ones. It scores which of them matter to it. Click any word and you will see what it looks at. A thicker line means a stronger link.

Verified: attention computes how strongly each token ties to the others, and mixes their vectors accordingly. Simplified: these values were picked by hand to fit the intuition. Real ones look less tidy.
6 / 20

Many heads

An attention head is one separate set of attention. Each head learns a different kind of link between words. A model has dozens of them in every layer. Below is the same token and three heads. Each one asks about something different.

Verified: heads specialise. Research into the inside of models finds heads for syntax, for position and for reference. Simplified: in a real model that division is not as clean as the labels suggest.
7 / 20

The transformer block and the stack

One block is attention, and after it a small dense network, marked FFN in the drawing. Both have a shortcut connection: the input skips the part and is added to its result. A model is dozens of such blocks stacked one on top of another. The model knows the position of a token only because the position arrives glued to the embedding.

Verified: the structure of the block, the shortcut connections, position encoding, the stack of blocks. Simplified: normalisation layers and variants are left out, for example RoPE (a different way of encoding position) and MoE (many specialised sub-networks).
Part III

Where the weights come from

Nobody writes the weights by hand. They come out of one number: the error. And one operation: a step downhill.

8 / 20

Loss

The model predicts how likely each token is to come next. The loss says how badly it was wrong: the less probability it gave the correct token, the bigger the penalty. Click which token was the correct one.

Start of the sentence: "The capital of France is" → the model predicted:
loss = −ln(p of the correct token). Training looks for weights that push this number down on average across billions of examples.
Verified: loss computed this way (cross-entropy) is the standard in pretraining. Pretraining is the first and largest stage of training. Simplified: a distribution over 6 tokens instead of the whole vocabulary.
9 / 20

Gradient

The gradient is the slope of the loss with respect to one weight. It says which way to move the weight so that the loss goes down. Click "Step". Then set the step size to the maximum and watch what happens.

0.30
Verified: the gradient is a derivative. The step goes in the direction opposite to the gradient. Too long a step makes training diverge. Simplified: one smooth curve instead of a surface with billions of dimensions. Real optimisers, the programs that steer the step (Adam for example), add momentum and set the step size themselves.
10 / 20

Backpropagation

The loss appears at the end of the model. The gradient, though, is needed for the weights in the first layer too. Backpropagation computes it with the chain rule: the error flows backwards through every layer and is multiplied along the way.

Verified: backprop is the chain rule applied layer by layer. The computer works it out on its own. Simplified: the formulas are left out.
11 / 20

Pretraining, post-training, fine-tuning

The same mechanism (loss, gradient, step) run on different data gives different models. A raw model after pretraining can only finish text. What makes it an assistant is post-training, further training on examples of conversations and on human ratings. Fine-tuning is the tuning of a finished model on your own examples. The second table compares it with supplying knowledge in the context, in the text sent together with the question. RAG is the automatic picking of document fragments for that text (section 17).

Verified: the split into pretraining and post-training. Fine-tuning teaches style better, RAG delivers facts better. Logical, no number: whether fine-tuning pays off depends on the number of queries.
Part IV

When you type

What happens from the moment you press Enter to the last token of the answer. The weights no longer change.

12 / 20

Context window

The context window is everything the model sees at one moment. Instructions, documents, the conversation history and your prompt all go in there as one sequence of tokens. The prompt is the text you send to the model. The model does not see files or messages, only that sequence. Click "Next".

InstructionsDocumentsConversationQuestionAnswer
Verified: the model has one window and processes all of it at once. From message to message it remembers nothing. The answer is built token by token. Simplified: the attention arcs are illustrative. How the history gets cut depends on the application.
13 / 20

KV cache

Every new token has to look at all the previous ones. Without a cache the model would compute them again every single time. The KV cache stores the results for the old tokens. The price is memory, which grows along with the length of the context.

40k tokens
Without cacheWith cache
Operations per 1 new token
Cache memory (rough)0
This is why a long context is slower and more expensive. Providers also store the repeating openings of conversations. That service is called prompt caching.
Verified: during generation the KV cache turns a quadratic cost into a linear one. Without it, twice the context means four times the computation, with it, twice. Memory grows at the same rate as the context. Simplified: the memory figures are an order of magnitude, they depend on the model.
14 / 20

Softmax and temperature

At the end the model has one number for every token in the vocabulary. Softmax is the formula that turns them into probabilities. Temperature is the randomness setting. A low one sharpens the distribution and the model almost always picks the same thing, a high one flattens it. Then a token is drawn at random. That is why the same conversation gives different answers.

Context: "The capital of France is"
1.0
Verified: softmax, temperature, drawing a token (sampling). Simplified: 6 candidates instead of the whole vocabulary. Products add filters, for example top-p, which cuts off the least likely tokens.
15 / 20

Hallucinations

A hallucination is a confident sounding answer that nothing backs up. A missing fact does not raise an error message. The weights know the shape of the answer, so the model writes something plausible. Switch whether the fact is in the context.

Verified: a hallucination follows from the model writing the most likely continuation. It is not an "error" in the ordinary sense. Post-training teaches the model to say "I do not know", but it does not remove the mechanism.

17 / 20

RAG

When there are too many documents for the window, a separate search engine picks the matching fragments and glues them into the window. That is RAG. The model sees only what got in. Top-k is the number of fragments picked. Switch between the questions and move the slider.

3
ProblemSymptom
A bad split of the documentThe answer cut across two fragments, the model sees half of it
k too smallContext is missing, the model guesses or says it does not know
k too largeNoise, attention blurs
Codes, numbers, proper namesVectors catch the sense, not exact strings. Hence the pairing with keyword search
Verified: a document fragment (chunk) → vector → similarity → top-k. In products the model itself decides when to search and with what (agentic retrieval). Simplified: two dimensions instead of thousands. No reranker, the second sieve that orders the results once more.
18 / 20

Tools and the agent

The model has no hands. What it has is a trained habit: instead of answering, it can write out a tool call. A tool is a function with a description, which the application runs at the request of the model. The application performs the call and pastes the result back into the window. The loop runs until the model writes an answer. A model in such a loop is an agent.

WhatHow it looks to the model
Searching the webThe tool returns fragments of pages as text in the window
Running codeThe model writes code, gets the result as text
MemoryA file of facts about you, pasted in or read on demand
Searching the chat historyThe tool returns fragments of old conversations
Verified: tool calls are text generated by the model and executed by the application, the result comes back into the window. Important: every tool result is tokens, which use up the window. Three rules that follow from this

RuleApplies to conversationsWhat to do
The more the question is "yours", the more the answer depends on what you paste in. The weights give only a skeleton.4, 7, 8, 9Paste the data, do not describe it. Numbers, not "roughly".
The more the question is about the world today, the less you should trust the weights.5, 6Force a search. Ask for sources and dates.
The more the question is philosophical and carries your own thesis, the bigger the risk. The model will build a house on your foundation without checking the foundation.1, 10Ask for the weakest point of the thesis first. Only then for the expansion.
Verified: models after RLHF adapt to the frame of the question. This is a documented effect, called sycophancy. Logical: the three rules are a conclusion drawn from the mechanism, not the result of a study.
